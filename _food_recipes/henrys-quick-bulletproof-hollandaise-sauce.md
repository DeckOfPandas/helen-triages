<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Why Hollandaise is hard</span></h2>

Hollandaise is a metastable fat-in-water emulsion. You are forcing a preposterous amount of hot butter to suspend itself inside the water in the egg yolk and acids. The sauce stays kinetically trapped only because the energy barrier to separating is high, but this still leaves an uncomfortably narrow margin for error.

And a note on food safety: this is a warm, barely-cooked egg yolk sauce meant to be held at serving temperature for a while. Don't hold it too long in the temperature "danger zone" range, 8°C to 63°C, before serving. Below 8°C, bacterial growth is slow enough to be safe. Above 63°C, heat kills the bacteria.

<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Tips</span></h2>

- **Use a heavy-bottomed saucepan for the butter**. If it gets away from you and browns, you won't end up with Hollandaise, but maybe something from an achingly cool hipster café with things like "deconstructed organic sunlight in a Kilner jar" on the menu for £34. But in a bad way.
- **Trust the science of cooling**. It hasn't failed to set until it's finger-warm and still not set. 
- **Whisk manually with a balloon whisk for better control of texture**. You want a classic, glossy, velvety finish rather than over-aerated foam. There's a higher chance of splitting with an electric whisk because the high speed can break the emulsion before it has had time to form, compromising stability. Furthermore, with an electric beater you'll have poor bowl contact compared with the manual approach. Think of it as free gym. 
- **Use clarified butter**. Whole butter carries in about 15% water and milk solids before you've even started — you can get away with it, but you're gifting your already-narrow emulsion even less room for error. Clarifying your butter first gives a slightly bigger safety net.
- **Salt helps:** the sodium and chloride ions slightly alter the protein matrix, helping it hold water better — one more reason not to skip seasoning. Unsalted butter plus salt added directly, rather than however much happens to be in salted butter, means you can actually control that margin — and dial it back if you're serving this over something already salty, like bacon or ham.

<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Troubleshooting</span></h2>
<p class="recipe-section-subtitle">a.k.a. egg yolks are sneaky</p>


Here are four ways the emulsion can fail:

1. **Splitting: a greasy yellow slick floating on top of flabby egg mush**
   - **Impatience:** You added the butter too quickly. The low-density lipoproteins (LDLs) in the egg yolk couldn't envelop the fat droplets in time, causing the lipids to separate.
   - **Inattention:** You let the butter boil. It cooked the egg proteins on contact due to sheer volume, instantly destroying their ability to emulsify.

2. **Slackness: a thin, watery liquid that refuses to hold its shape**
   - **Lack of commitment:** Your acid wasn't boiling when it hit the yolks. A flash of heat lowers the viscosity of the yolk, allowing you to beat it into smaller droplets, while also supplying the jumpstart of energy needed to partially unfold the LDLs. The amount of hot acid here is small compared to the amount of cold yolk, so you won't cook the eggs by mistake.  
   - **Insufficient violence:** You didn't whisk enough. If you don't break the fat into microscopic droplets, large droplets slide past each other, and the sauce will never thicken.

3. **Curdling: a lumpy, grainy texture like wet sand**
   - **Poor forward-planning:** The bain-marie was too hot while waiting to serve. Eggs coagulate permanently at 65°C. Given enough time at a high temperature, you'll have a protein disaster, where proteins slowly aggregate into visible lumps. Note the word "permanently".

4. **Overcooking: a stodgy, gluey sauce that stands up like wallpaper paste**
   - **Drought:** You used too little acid, or over-clarified butter. There was nothing but fat and protein left to do the work, so the sauce set thick and pasty instead of staying pourable.
   - **Confusion with other brunch:** You followed (clumsy) advice to slowly warm the egg yolks over low heat before adding the butter. The cooked proteins lost the flexibility to stretch and coat the fat droplets properly, so instead of smooth thickening you got weird blancmange.

### A final warning

- **Try to avoid reheating once cooled:** If you try to reheat cold, set Hollandaise, you'll have a lipid disaster. The butter matrix melts rapidly, lowering viscosity, and this increased thermal motion causes the microscopic fat droplets to collide and coalesce into giant blobs of oil. Because the protein barrier was already fixed into structure when you first made the sauce, it cannot stretch to coat the expanding surface area of the merging fat blobs, and your sauce splits.
