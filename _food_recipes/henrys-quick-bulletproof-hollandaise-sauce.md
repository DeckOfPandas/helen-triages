<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Why Hollandaise is hard</span></h2>

Hollandaise sauce is a metastable fat-in-water emulsion. We need to force a preposterous amount of hot butter to suspend itself inside the water in the egg yolk and acids. There is an uncomfortably narrow margin for error.

And a note on food safety: this starts out as raw egg. Don't let it sit too long in the temperature "danger zone" range, 8°C to 63°C, before serving.

<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Tips</span></h2>

- **Use a heavy-bottomed saucepan for the butter.** If the butter gets away and browns, hide the eggs and tell everyone they're having beurre noisette instead.
- **Trust the science of cooling.** It hasn't failed to set until it's finger-warm and still failed to set. 
- **Whisk manually with a balloon whisk for better control of texture.** We want a classic, glossy, velvety finish rather than over-aerated foam. There's a higher chance of splitting with an electric whisk, and a whisk gives poor bowl contact compared with doing it by hand. Put "Eye of the Tiger" on and get to it.
- **Use clarified butter.** Whole butter brings in about 15% water and milk solids. Clarifying the butter first gives a slightly bigger safety net for the emulsion to form.
- **Salt helps.** The sodium and chloride ions slightly alter the protein matrix, helping it hold water better. Unsalted butter plus salt added directly, rather than however much happens to be in salted butter, gives some control over that margin.

<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Troubleshooting</span></h2>
<p class="recipe-section-subtitle">a.k.a. egg yolks are sneaky</p>


Here are four ways the emulsion can fail:

1. **Splitting** *a greasy yellow slick floating on top of flabby egg mush*
   - **The butter went in too quickly.** There's a limit to how fast egg yolk can coat butter.
   - **The butter boiled.** It cooked the egg proteins on contact due to sheer volume, destroying their ability to emulsify.

2. **Slackness** *a thin, watery liquid that refuses to hold its shape*
   - **The acid wasn't boiling when it hit the yolks.** Cold acid can't soften the egg proteins enough to beat into small droplets.
   - **Insufficient violence.** Whisking fail. If fat isn't beaten into microscopic droplets, large droplets slide past each other, and the sauce will never thicken.

3. **Curdling** *a lumpy, grainy texture like wet sand*
   - **The bain-marie was too hot.** Eggs coagulate permanently at 65°C. Note the word "permanently".

4. **Overcooking** *a stodgy, gluey sauce that stands up like wallpaper paste*
   - **Too little acid, or over-clarified butter.** There was nothing but fat and protein left to do the work, so the sauce set thick and paste-y instead of staying pourable.
   - **Your heart wanted to make scrambled eggs instead.** You followed misplaced advice to slowly warm the egg yolks over low heat before adding the butter. The cooked proteins lost the flexibility to stretch and coat the fat droplets properly, so instead of smooth thickening you got weird blancmange.

<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">A Final Warning</span></h2>

**Reheating Hollandaise sauce is really hard**. Reheating cold, set Hollandaise clumsily means the fat melts too fast, then giant blobs of lipids will split the sauce because the egg can't keep up.

If reheating is a must, do so very very slowly over a bain-marie, throw some salt over your left shoulder, and stir very gently but continuously.
