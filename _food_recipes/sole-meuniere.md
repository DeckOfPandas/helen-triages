<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">On Poissons à la Meunière</span></h2>

<p class="recipe-section-subtitle">Mostly from Elizabeth David, who suspects you have been badly advised</p>

Sole meunière is sometimes described as "one of the simplest dishes there is". This is ridiculous. Simple in concept does not mean simple in execution. 

Recipes invariably leave out the key details that determine whether or not this dish works. 


<h2 class="recipe-section-heading"><span class="section-heading-text">Traps and how to avoid them</span></h2>

- **Fry in clarified butter.** Milk solids can burn before the fish is cooked.
- **Don't reuse the frying butter as the finishing butter.** The frying butter is dead once used. Discard it, then brown fresh (this time unclarified) butter in a clean pan for finishing, giving flavour and foam.
- **Stop heating the finishing butter when it's brown enough.** Pale hazelnut, not darker, no exceptions. The clue is in "noisette".
- **Don't let the fish sit once the finishing butter has been poured.** Butter and the fish should reach the table together, still foaming. The hot butter will continue to cook the fish, while also turning cold and unattractive itself, with sad, soggy capers like accusing icebergs.
- **Cook just two portions at a time in one ordinary pan.** Batching leaves the first portions turning cold and soggy. Use more pans if you need more than two portions, or cook something else. (Or acquire a giant pro pan and three-burner hob.)
- **Treat expensive fish with respect.** Poor technique and timing here wastes good sole, so don't just assume this is easy. Watch the pan like a hawk, and do nothing else at the same time.
