Cooking methods and timings: the original page prose

Chicken

Whole bird, roasting methods

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing (per kg) · Temp change · Covering · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight calculus: the "X min/kg + 20 min" formulas (standard, high-then-low, foil-tented, fully-wrapped) hold reliably for birds roughly 1.3 kg to 3 kg. Below ~1.3 kg the flat +20 min addition becomes disproportionately large relative to cook time — use a flat total time (~1 hr to 1 hr 10 min) and check temperature. Above ~3 kg, heat penetration to the thigh becomes the bottleneck, so larger birds need a lower temperature (160–170°C) for a longer, gentler cook.

Sources, after the table: Sources: standard/high-heat-start rates match BBC Good Food and Delia Smith's common "20 min per 450g + 20 min" formula (≈44 min/kg+20min). Low-and-slow and roasting-bag corrections and the spatchcock rewrite are from Reynolds' own oven-bag timing chart and comparable recipes (Bell & Evans and others) for a whole spatchcocked bird at 220–230°C. Foil-tented/fully-wrapped weren't specifically re-checked this pass — same weight-calculus caveat as everything above. Checked once, 2026-08-12 — tidy/remove these notes once you've confirmed a row yourself.

Turkey

Whole bird, roasting methods

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Weight range · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight edge cases: below ~4 kg, treat like a very large chicken — check from the low end of the timing. Above ~10 kg, per-kg formulas break down; use a much lower oven temperature (140–150°C) and time by thermometer alone, checking from about two-thirds of the naive estimate onward.

Sources, after the table: Sources: standard rate confirmed against four independent UK sources (Jamie Oliver, Delia Smith, BBC Good Food, Butterball/USDA) all converging on ~35–44 min/kg for a whole unstuffed bird — the old ~20 min/kg figure undercooked a 5 kg turkey by roughly an hour against your own recipe's actual 2½ hr/30 min-per-kg result, which is itself a useful cross-check against this table. High-heat-start rate from Mary Berry's method. Roasting-bag rate from Reynolds' own oven-bag chart (the fastest of the enclosed methods, used as the anchor for the tented/wrapped estimates above it, which weren't independently sourced). Spatchcock rate from Bon Appétit-style conversions. Tented/wrapped/low-and-slow rows are estimates by analogy, not independently confirmed — check these first if you cook a turkey this way. Checked once, 2026-08-12 — tidy/remove these notes once you've confirmed a row yourself.

Goose

Whole bird, roasting methods

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Weight range · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight edge cases: geese vary less in weight than turkeys (commercially usually 3.5–6.5 kg), so the per-kg formulas are reasonably reliable across the typical range. Below ~3.5 kg, treat like a very large duck and check earlier than the formula suggests.

Sources, after the table: Sources: standard and high-heat-start rates from Delia Smith's own goose recipe (5.4 kg bird, 30 min blast + 3 hrs at 180°C ≈ 39 min/kg), Gordon Ramsay's recipe (220°C blast 25–30 min, drop to 170°C, then 30 min/kg), and Abel & Cole (same pattern). Low-and-slow rate from genuine 150°C/300°F goose recipes running 3–4 hrs for a ~5 kg bird. Tented and steaming rows are estimates by analogy, not independently sourced. Checked once, 2026-08-12 — tidy/remove these notes once you've confirmed a row yourself.

Duck

Whole bird, roasting methods

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Weight range · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight edge cases: most whole ducks sold are 1.5–2.5 kg. Below ~1.5 kg the flat +20 min addition becomes disproportionate — treat like a very large poussin and check early. Above ~3 kg is unusual for domestic duck; use a lower temperature (150–160°C) for longer to render fat fully without scorching the skin.

Sources, after the table: Sources: Gressingham's own roasting guide and Great British Chefs for the standard/high-heat rates. Nigella's slow-roast duck method for the low-and-slow row (indirect comparison, different temperature — treat as a wide estimate, not a confirmed figure). Spatchcock range reflects genuinely conflicting US vs UK sources rather than one being simply wrong — this is the one row on this page where the disagreement is between real recipes, not a unit-conversion error. Duck is the protein least affected by the systematic error found elsewhere on this page. Checked once, 2026-08-12 — tidy/remove these notes once you've confirmed a row yourself.

Tough/working cuts — brisket, chuck, short rib, pot roast

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp/covering · Liquid · Notes

Note, before the table: Cooked low and slow until tender, not to a doneness temperature — timing below is a planning estimate only.

Weight, after the table: Weight range: holds well 1.5–4.5 kg. Below 1.5 kg, check early to avoid overshooting into dryness. Above 4.5–5 kg (full packer brisket), go by tenderness rather than extrapolating the formula. Endpoint temperature: see temperature charts.

Sources, after the table: Sources: collagen-breakdown temperature (90–96°C, on the temperature charts) and short rib/pot roast timings confirmed against standard BBQ/braise references — 195–205°F (90–96°C) is the widely-cited fall-apart range, and ~300°F/149°C for ~3 hrs matches typical short rib recipes. Brisket low-and-slow rate corrected against widely-cited BBQ/oven guidance for the same ~121°C bracket. Checked once, 2026-08-12 — tidy/remove these notes once you've confirmed a row yourself.

Pork

Roasting cuts — loin, leg/fresh ham, rack/crown roast, tenderloin

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight range: holds well 1.5–4.5 kg (loin/leg). Below 1.5 kg (or tenderloin generally), check well before the formula suggests. Above 4.5–5 kg, drop toward the low-temp end of the range. Doneness temperatures: see temperature charts.

Food safety, after the table: FOOD SAFETY: the doneness figures this timing table cooks toward (see temperature charts) include a "medium, slightly pink" option at 60–62°C that does not clear the UK FSA's own core-temperature guidance for pork specifically — unlike beef or lamb, FSA doesn't treat pink pork the same as pink beef. Full detail on the temperature charts; flagging here too since this is the page you'd actually be timing against mid-cook.

Sources, after the table: Sources: Good To Know and Village Butchers for the leg/crackling rate (~30 min/500g = ~60 min/kg after a blast). US-style guidance (~40 min/kg at 175–180°C) for lean loin — kept as a separate row rather than overwriting the leg rate, since the two cuts genuinely cook at different speeds. Waitrose's roast guide and a BBC pork fillet recipe for the tenderloin correction. Low-temp reverse-sear rate confirmed against Kenji López-Alt/Serious Eats practice, unchanged. Checked once, 2026-08-12 — tidy/remove these notes once you've confirmed a row yourself.

Slow-cooked cuts — shoulder/pork butt, belly

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Covering · Notes

Note, before the table: Cooked past the "done" temperature until fat and collagen render — timing by tenderness, not just weight.

Weight, after the table: Weight range: holds well 2–5 kg. Below 2 kg, check earlier. Above 5–6 kg, go by tenderness rather than extrapolating.

Sources, after the table: Sources: collagen breakdown at 90–95°C (temperature charts page) is the standard fall-apart range. The shoulder timing spread reflects real recipes at very different target textures rather than one being wrong — a 1.6 kg shoulder at 150°C for 6 hrs and a 100°C/12hr version were both used as reference points. Only the two "shoulder, low and slow" rows were re-checked this pass; covered/uncovered and belly weren't specifically tested. Ham

Fresh (uncured, raw) ham — bone-in or boneless

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight range: holds for 2–5 kg (bone-in) or 1.8–4.5 kg (boneless). Doneness temperatures: see temperature charts.

Food safety, after the table: FOOD SAFETY: a fresh ham is pork leg, so the same flag applies as pork above — the "hint of pink" doneness option (60–62°C) on the temperature charts page does not clear FSA's pork-specific guidance. The well-done option does.

Sources, after the table: Sources: this table's timing rates were mostly confirmed as-is against UK guides (~30–40 min/kg at 180°C is the common range; a 220–230°C blast for 20–30 min then 180°C at ~30–40 min/kg also matches typical practice) — no changes made here, unlike the cured table below. Cured (and/or smoked) whole ham — bone-in or boneless

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Notes

Note, before the table: Assumes a raw-cured ham that still needs cooking — check packaging first: "cook before eating" and "ready to eat" are handled differently.

Weight, after the table: Weight range: cured hams usually sold 2–4.5 kg bone-in. Doneness pull temp: see temperature charts (target corrected to 70–75°C — was 68–72°C, a touch under both Waitrose's and FSA's figures).

Caveat, after the table: Caveat: cured ham labelling varies a lot (unsmoked vs smoked, wet vs dry-cured, cook-before-eating vs ready-to-eat) — check packaging instructions against the thermometer reading rather than relying on generic figures.

Sources, after the table: Sources: Waitrose, LovePork, Morrisons and flawlessfood all converge on the ~45 min/kg boil rate and ~60 min/kg + 30 min roast-only rate ("30 min per 500g plus 30" is the exact UK rule of thumb). This is the discrepancy that best matches your own memory of a lb/kg-style mix-up from an earlier pass — both figures here were roughly half the sourced rate, in exactly the shape a dropped unit conversion produces. Venison

Roasting cuts — haunch, loin, saddle, rack

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Temp change · Covering · Notes

Weight, after the table: Weight range: holds well for haunches 1.3–2.5 kg, which is most of what is sold. Below that, or for loin, saddle and rack generally, time by the piece and check early — venison is lean enough that the margin between pink and grey is a few minutes. Doneness temperatures: see temperature charts.

Sources, after the table: Sources: Sainsbury's Magazine and Great British Recipes for the high-heat-start rates. That row is the one place on this table where the fan figure is stated rather than converted by us — Great British Recipes prints "200 FAN" then "160 FAN", Sainsbury's Magazine prints "fan 200°C" then "fan 140°C", which is why the drop is given as a range rather than a single figure. Church Farm Oxford for the constant roast, the Wild Meat Company for the foil-wrapped row, and Farmfetch for the rack and the loin. Eversfield Organic's 20 mins per 500 g is an outlier — roughly 1.6× the other three at the same oven temperature — so it is cited here rather than averaged in.

Slow-cooked cuts — shoulder, neck, shank

Table columns: Method · Oven temp · Timing · Covering · Liquid · Notes

Note, before the table: Cooked past any doneness temperature until the collagen gives way — the timing below is a planning estimate, not a target.

Weight, after the table: Weight range: shoulders are usually 1.5–3 kg. Shanks are sold by the piece and timed that way, so no per-kg rate applies to them. Endpoint temperature: see temperature charts.

Sources, after the table: Sources: the Wild Meat Company's pulled venison for the low-and-slow row, with the Great British Food Awards for its oven temperature — it prints "170°C/Fan 150°C", so that is a stated fan figure rather than one we converted. Farmfetch for the pot roast. Shooting UK, Great British Chefs and the Wild Meat Company for the shanks: the 2–3 hr spread between them is real disagreement about how big a shank is, not a rounding of one figure. Neck has no row of its own because no UK source publishes a venison neck timing — every neck recipe found was American — so it is folded in with the shoulder rows, at the same rate, and the group name says so.
