Sustainability

Directional guidance, not a fixed ruling — ratings move, vary by exact source, and this page won't be kept in step with every update. For a specific purchase, the MCS Good Fish Guide or Seafood Checker app will give the rating for that exact source.

Fish

FishQuick readNotes
SalmonCheck, expect not okay (farmed)Wild Atlantic salmon is rare and often catch-restricted; most UK salmon is farmed, with known issues around sea lice, escapees, and local ecosystem impact. Trout is considered a more sustainable substitute.
Cod / haddockCod: avoid. Haddock: check, expect okayUK cod has recently been downgraded to the worst possible sustainability rating due to sharp stock decline — hake is now recommended as the more sustainable alternative. Haddock is generally sustainable, though often catches cod as bycatch — best from the North Sea or West of Scotland.
Sea bassCheck, expect okayRatings have recently improved in several regions (North Sea, English Channel, Bay of Biscay) due to better management and stock recovery, though some fisheries remain lower-rated.
Sea breamCheck, expect okayGenerally moderate; farmed bream has a smaller footprint than many wild white fish, but farming method matters.
TroutGenerally okayConsidered the more sustainable alternative to salmon, and widely recommended as a swap for it.
MackerelAvoidRecently downgraded to red-rated and pulled from shelves by several UK retailers — a reversal from its former reputation as a sustainable choice.
HalibutCheck, expect not okayWild Atlantic halibut is slow-growing and often overfished; farmed is generally better.
TurbotGenerally okayAdded to the green-rated "Best Choice" list in recent updates.
PlaiceGenerally okayNorth Sea stocks are currently booming, one of the better white-fish choices.
SoleCheck, expect okaySimilar family to plaice but more variable by source.
TunaCheck, could go either waySpans both sustainable (pole-and-line skipjack) and red-rated (bigeye, some purse-seine) depending on species and method — also has documented human-rights concerns in parts of the distant-water fleet.
SwordfishCheck, expect not okayLong-lived, slow-reproducing; often longline-caught with significant bycatch of sharks and turtles.
MonkfishCheck, expect not okayOften bottom-trawled, which can damage seabed habitats.
Red mulletCheck, could go either waySome fisheries have moved onto the red "Avoid" list while others remain fine — no blanket answer.
John DoryCheck, could go either wayTypically landed as bycatch of trawl fisheries, so its footprint follows whatever fishery catches it.
HerringGenerally okayFast-growing, short-lived, historically well-managed in UK/North Sea stocks, though this varies by area.
SkateCheck, expect not okaySeveral species are slow-growing and were historically overfished to local extinction in UK waters — buy only clearly-specied, rated skate.

General caveat: these are directional, not fixed — ratings are updated roughly twice a year and vary by exact fishing ground and method within the same species. The UK also imports around 80% of the seafood it consumes, so don't assume "local" for any of these without checking.

See also: fish cooking methods.

Shellfish

ShellfishQuick readNotes
Mussels (farmed, rope-grown)Generally okayConsidered Best Choice — rope-grown mussels actively benefit UK seas by filtering water, with very little habitat impact.
Oysters (farmed)Generally okayFarmed oysters are rated Best Choice alongside farmed mussels.
Clams / cocklesCheck, expect okayFarmed or hand-gathered options are generally good; some wild-dredged fisheries less so.
ScallopsCheck, could go either wayHand-dived scallops retain Best Choice status, and English farmed scallops have also been added to the Best Choice list — but dredged scallops (the more common commercial method) damage seabed habitats.
Prawns (UK farmed, closed-system)Generally okayNewly available UK land-farmed king prawns have a much lower environmental impact than typical imports.
Prawns (tropical, e.g. tiger/king, imported)Check, expect not okayMuch tropical prawn farming has historically been linked to mangrove destruction and, in some supply chains, labour abuse.
Langoustine (scampi)Check, could go either waySustainability depends heavily on catch method — pot- or creel-caught is a better choice than trawled, and some fisheries have recently been downgraded.
Crab (brown/edible, UK)Check, expect okayGenerally well-managed in UK waters, though this varies by fishery and season.
Lobster (European)Check, expect okayGenerally more sustainably managed than its American counterpart.
Lobster (American)AvoidRecently added to the Fish to Avoid list, largely due to pot-rope entanglement risk for the critically endangered North Atlantic right whale.
Squid (UK-caught)AvoidAdded to the red list — no management system in place and populations may be declining in several areas.
OctopusCheck, could go either wayIncreasing global demand has raised concern in some fisheries; well-managed Mediterranean and UK-bycatch sources are generally better than intensively-targeted ones.
WinklesGenerally okayTypically hand-gathered with low environmental impact.
CrawfishCheck, expect okayFarmed crawfish (common in Cajun-style supply) is generally reasonable; wild invasive-species crawfish (e.g. in parts of Europe) can even be a positive choice.
AbaloneCheck, expect not okayWild abalone is heavily overfished in many regions and often subject to strict catch controls or bans; farmed abalone is the safer choice where available.

General note: the shellfish picture is more favourable overall than the fin-fish one — low-impact shellfish are widely considered one of the better climate- and ocean-friendly protein sources available. The main things to watch for are catch method (dredged vs. hand-dived/potted) and origin (UK/European farmed vs. some imported tropical or American sources) rather than the species itself being inherently problematic.

See also: shellfish cooking methods.

Land Animal Meat — Carbon Impact

MeatApprox. carbon footprintQuick readNotes
Beef (beef-herd)~60–100 kg CO2e/kgBy far the highestThe most-cited figure is around 100 kg CO2eq per kg once food waste is factored in, though estimates as low as 60 kg appear before that adjustment — either way, several times higher than any other meat here. Driven mainly by methane from digestion and the large amount of land needed for grazing/feed.
Beef (dairy-herd, i.e. cull dairy cattle)~20–35 kg CO2e/kgHigh, but notably lower than beef-herd beefHistorically estimated at around 34 kg CO2eq per kg — roughly a third of dedicated beef-herd beef, since the animal's environmental cost is partly attributed to its dairy output instead.
Lamb / mutton~20–39 kg CO2e/kgHighEstimates vary a lot by methodology — a US-focused study put it at 39 kg CO2eq, while a global study based on Poore & Nemecek put it closer to 24 kg — either way, similar order of magnitude to beef, again largely methane-driven since sheep are also ruminants.
Pork~7–12 kg CO2e/kgModeratePigs aren't ruminants and produce far less methane, putting pork's footprint at under a third of beef's, though intensive farming practices still make it the third-highest-impact meat overall.
Chicken~6–7 kg CO2e/kgLow (among meats)Poultry has the smallest footprint of the major meats, averaging around 6 kg CO2eq per kg globally — efficient feed conversion and no methane from digestion are the main reasons.
Turkey~7–11 kg CO2e/kgLow–moderateEstimated at around 10.9 kg CO2eq per kg in at least one major analysis — similar territory to pork, and somewhat higher than chicken.
Duck / gooseLimited standardised data, generally between chicken and porkLow–moderate (estimated)Covered much less consistently in the standard datasets than the meats above — treat this as a rough placement rather than a solid figure.

Key takeaway: ruminants (beef and lamb) sit in a different tier entirely from everything else, mainly because of methane from digestion (enteric fermentation) plus the land footprint of grazing and feed crops. Even stripping out methane entirely, beef's footprint remains roughly four times that of chicken, so the gap isn't just a methane-accounting artefact. Pork, chicken, and turkey form a much closer cluster below that, with chicken consistently the lowest of the conventional meats.

Caveat on precision: carbon-footprint estimates for meat vary noticeably between studies depending on methodology (whether food waste, land-use change, or by-products are included), so treat the numbers as indicating scale and relative ranking rather than fixed values.

Nut Milks

Nut milkCarbon footprintWater useOther sustainability notesQuick read
AlmondLowVery highAlmond farming uses the most water of any common plant-milk ingredient — around 371 litres of water per litre of milk produced — and much of it is grown in water-stressed California, which matters more than the raw figure suggests. Also has a significant pesticide footprint, with documented harm to bee populations.Check, expect not okay (water/pesticides)
CashewLowModerate–highUses less water than almond but still more than legumes or seeds, and requires more land than almond. Often imported from India and Vietnam, adding transport emissions, and has documented labour concerns — dangerous hand de-shelling, with investigations uncovering forced labour in parts of the supply chain.Check, expect not okay (labour)
CoconutLowLowRelatively sustainable — hand-harvested without heavy machinery or significant chemical use.Generally okay
HazelnutLow (limited specific data)ModerateLess commonly studied than almond/cashew, but hazelnuts are generally less water-intensive tree nuts and mostly rain-fed in major growing regions (Turkey, Italy), unlike irrigation-dependent almonds.Check, expect okay
WalnutLow (limited specific data)Moderate–highAlso under-studied as a milk specifically, but walnut trees have meaningful water needs in some growing regions, similar in kind to almond if less extensively documented.Check, could go either way
MacadamiaLow (limited specific data)Moderate–highWater-intensive to grow and mostly imported (Australia, South Africa, Hawaii), adding transport emissions; niche enough that dedicated milk LCAs are scarce.Check, could go either way

The bigger picture: every nut milk here has a substantially lower carbon footprint and land footprint than dairy, so from a pure climate-emissions standpoint, all of them are a reasonable swap. The real differences show up on other metrics — water use and eutrophication — rather than carbon, which is why this table looks different in shape from the meat one: it's less "avoid the worst offenders" and more "pick your trade-off" (almond's water/pesticide cost vs. cashew's labour and transport cost vs. coconut's fairly clean profile but low protein and reliance on imports).

Practical note: where the ingredient is grown matters as much as which nut it is — almond milk's water footprint looks a lot worse when the almonds come from a water-stressed region like California than it does on paper, so checking sourcing (where available on packaging) adds real information beyond the nut type alone.
