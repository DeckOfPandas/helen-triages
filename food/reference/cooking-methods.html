Cooking Methods

How to cook a given protein several different ways, with the oven temperatures, rough timings and trade-offs for each. Timings are planning estimates, not a substitute for a thermometer — see internal temperatures for pull temps and endpoints.

Chicken

Standard constant roast180°C fan / 200°C conventional~40 min/kg + 20 minNoneUncoveredSimple, reliable; skin browns gradually. For 2.5 kg: ~2 hrs (the example here used to say 1 hr 40 min, which doesn't match the formula it's supposed to illustrate — 40×2.5+20=120 min, not 100)
High-heat start, then reduceStart 220°C, drop to 180°C after 20 min~40 min/kg + 20 min totalOne step downUncoveredKickstarts crisp skin, then finishes gently so it doesn't dry out

Foil-tented roast190–200°C~40 min/kg + 20 minNoneFoil loosely over breast for first 2/3 of cook, removed for last 20–30 minProtects breast from drying/over-browning while legs catch up
Fully foil-wrapped (steam-roast)180°C~40 min/kg + 20 min, foil removed for last 20–25 minNoneFully wrapped, then opened at the endVery moist, almost pot-roast texture; skin won't crisp until unwrapped
Roasting bag190–200°C (check bag manufacturer's max)~33–44 min/kg (bags cook slightly faster than open roasting, but not as fast as this used to claim)NoneSealed in bag throughout, slit open for last 10–15 min if you want colourFast, very moist, minimal oven mess; skin stays pale unless opened at the end
Spatchcocked (flattened)220°C~40 min total for a typical 1.5–2 kg birdNoneUncoveredMuch faster and more even than trussed roasting; not "whole" in shape but often grouped with whole-bird methods. Given as a total time, not min/kg — flattening changes the geometry enough that a per-kg rate doesn't scale sensibly (the previous "~12–15 min/kg" here didn't even match its own "~30–35 min total" claim, aside from being too fast)

Weight calculus: the "X min/kg + 20 min" formulas (standard, high-then-low, foil-tented, fully-wrapped) hold reliably for birds roughly 1.3 kg to 3 kg. Below ~1.3 kg the flat +20 min addition becomes disproportionately large relative to cook time — use a flat total time (~1 hr to 1 hr 10 min) and check temperature. Above ~3 kg, heat penetration to the thigh becomes the bottleneck, so larger birds need a lower temperature (160–170°C) for a longer, gentler cook.

Sources: standard/high-heat-start rates match BBC Good Food and Delia Smith's common "20 min per 450g + 20 min" formula (≈44 min/kg+20min). Low-and-slow and roasting-bag corrections and the spatchcock rewrite are from Reynolds' own oven-bag timing chart and comparable recipes (Bell & Evans and others) for a whole spatchcocked bird at 220–230°C. Foil-tented/fully-wrapped weren't specifically re-checked this pass — same weight-calculus caveat as everything above. Method summary:

  • Pan-sear: hot pan, high smoke-point fat, short cook, rest after
  • Grill/BBQ: direct high heat, similar timing logic to pan-sear
  • Reverse sear: low oven (100–120°C) until close to target temp, then a very hot short sear for the crust — best for thick cuts
  • Pan-sear + oven finish: sear both sides on the stove, then finish in a hot oven (200–220°C)

Cross-cutting methods (apply across several fish/forms above):

  • En papillote: most white fish fillets (cod, haddock, sea bass, sea bream, plaice)
  • Deep-frying (battered): cod, haddock, plaice fillets
  • Ceviche/raw: sea bass, sea bream, tuna

Timing rule of thumb (governed by thickness, not weight): pan-fry/grill ~3–4 min per cm thickness per side; oven-bake at 220–230°C ~4 min per cm thickness total (~10 min per inch). Endpoint is opaque and flaking — see the FOOD SAFETY note below on what temperature that actually corresponds to. Salmon and tuna are commonly pulled well before that for a rare or pink centre; see internal temperatures for both.

Correction, 2026-08-12: the oven-bake rule used to say "~10 min per cm" — this looks like the well-known Canadian "10-minute rule" (10 min per inch of thickness at 450°F/232°C) with inches silently read as centimetres, which makes the old figure roughly 2.5× too long. A 3 cm fillet needs ~12 min by the correct rule, not ~30.

FOOD SAFETY: the "opaque and flaking" endpoint most home cooks actually use is lower than the official benchmark — USDA gives 145°F/62.8°C, and UK FSA guidance points to the same ~63°C. Salmon pulled at 52–55°C for a moist centre (a common chef preference — see internal temperatures) is below that mark; it's a texture choice, not the safety figure, and worth knowing the difference between the two rather than treating "looks done" as equivalent to "meets the guideline."
